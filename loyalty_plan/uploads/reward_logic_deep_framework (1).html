Reward logic deep framework

Psychology → loop structure → what/when/how to reward → blueprint per restaurant type

The 5 psychological forces you must design around

Force 1
Variable reward schedule — the dopamine engine
B.F. Skinner's 1950s research proved that unpredictable rewards create more persistent behavior than fixed rewards. A rat pressing a lever for random food portions pressed far more than one getting the same amount every time. The brain's dopamine system responds more powerfully to anticipation of a reward than the reward itself — this is why slot machines are addictive and why Zomato's scratch cards work. Fixed rewards (5 visits = free coffee) are predictable — useful for habit formation but they plateau. Variable rewards (spin to win, surprise bonus, mystery reward at random milestones) create compulsion.
How to use this
Layer both: fixed loops for habit formation + variable surprises layered on top. Example: the 5-visit coffee loop is fixed (safety, predictable value). But on visit 3, a surprise "bonus reward unlocked" fires. Customer didn't expect it. Dopamine spike. They tell their friend. They come back faster.
Force 2
Endowed progress effect — the head start trick
Nunes & Drèze (2006) gave 300 car wash customers two different loyalty cards. Group A: 10 stamps needed, 2 already filled in. Group B: 8 stamps needed, none filled in. Both required 8 actual stamps. Result: Group A completed at 34% vs Group B at 19%. The only difference was the illusion of a head start. Humans are more motivated to complete something that's already begun than to start something new. This is why LinkedIn shows "your profile is 40% complete" — you feel obligated to finish.
How to use this
Never show a customer an empty progress bar. On first scan, their milestone is already at 1/5 — "Your first visit counted!" The loop started the moment they walked in. This single tweak can increase loop completion by 50–80% based on research.
Force 3
Goal gradient effect — acceleration near the finish
Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng (2006) showed that people accelerate their behavior as they get closer to a goal. Coffee shop customers visited more frequently as they approached their free drink milestone. The closer to the goal, the faster they moved. This is why progress bars are so powerful — they make the gap feel closeable. When a customer is at 4/5 visits, they will actively think "I should go this week" in a way they never did at 1/5.
How to use this
Send a WhatsApp trigger at 60% and 80% of every loop: "You're 2 visits away from your free dessert." This is the highest-ROI message you will ever send. It activates goal gradient acceleration at exactly the right moment.
Force 4
Loss aversion — status you don't want to lose
Kahneman showed losses feel 2× more painful than equivalent gains feel good. Once a customer has earned status or progress, they will work harder to keep it than to get it in the first place. Starbucks Gold status works not because of what it gives you — but because losing it feels worse than the perks feel good. This is also why expiring points create urgency: "Your reward expires in 3 days" outperforms "You have a reward available" every single time.
How to use this
Set reward expiry dates (30–60 days is ideal). Send expiry warning 5 days before. Use language of loss: "Your free dessert expires Friday" not "Don't forget your reward." Also: once a customer completes a journey and unlocks Level 2, remind them what they'd lose if they stop — their unlocked journey, their status, their streak.
Force 5
Reciprocity & social identity — "I'm a regular here"
When a restaurant gives someone a reward, the customer feels a subconscious obligation to return — not because they're calculating value, but because being recognized as a regular is a part of their self-identity. "This is my place" is more powerful than any discount. Starbucks nails this by knowing your name and order. The goal is to make the customer feel the restaurant knows them — and that loyalty is about belonging, not just getting free stuff.
How to use this
Personalize every touchpoint with the customer's name. "Hey Priya, you're 1 away from your free cocktail" feels completely different from "Dear customer." Also, reward non-transactional milestones: birthday, restaurant anniversary ("You've been with us for 6 months!"). These create identity attachment, not just transactional loyalty.

The 4-layer loop system — how to stack engagement

L1
The entry loop — hook fast
First scan → first reward. Must complete within 2–3 visits.
This is the only loop most restaurants think about. It's the shortest, cheapest, and most important. Its only job is to prove value quickly enough that the customer bothers coming back. The reward here is low-cost but immediate. The psychology is endowed progress + reciprocity — "they already gave me something, and I'm almost done."
Structure
5 visits → free item of restaurant's choice (coffee, dessert, side dish — high perceived value, low actual cost).
Start customer at visit 1/5 on first scan (endowed progress).
Reward fires automatically on visit 5 — no staff involvement needed.
Total cost to restaurant: ₹80–₹150. Value to build habit: enormous.
L2
The engagement loop — earn through action
Behavior-based rewards. Unlocks after L1 completes.
Once a customer has proven they come back, the engagement loop activates. This is where your activity system lives — reviewing, sharing, referring. These behaviors have direct value to the restaurant (social proof, new customers) so the reward can and should be higher. Critically: this loop runs in parallel with L3, not instead of it. Completing activities doesn't replace visits — it supplements them.
Structure
Each activity unlocks a specific reward — not a generic "points." The reward should match the behavior's value to the restaurant.

Leave a Google review → free drink Share an Instagram story → 20% off next visit Refer a friend who visits → free starter Post a reel → exclusive invite to a private tasting Birthday visit → complimentary dessert
L3
The habit loop — build a ritual
Category-specific repeat behavior. This is where true retention lives.
This is the deepest retention layer — building category-specific rituals. Not just "visit 5 times" but "drink 5 cold brews" or "order brunch 3 Sundays." This loop targets the specific behavior the restaurant wants to reinforce — and it evolves. Once a brunch loop completes, a cocktail evening loop unlocks. The customer never runs out of progress to make. This is the Duolingo streak mechanic applied to dining.
Structure — evolving loops
Loop 1 (entry habit): Any 5 visits → free item. Simple, accessible.
Loop 2 (category habit): 3 weekend brunches → signature dish free. Builds specific day-of-week habit.
Loop 3 (product habit): 5 cocktails ordered → cocktail masterclass invite. Deepens product engagement.
Loop 4 (ambassador): Bring 3 new people who join loyalty → exclusive member dinner invite.
Each loop unlocks the next. Customer always has a journey active.
L4
The status loop — identity and belonging
Long-term recognition. Makes loyalty a social identity.
The rarest customers become brand ambassadors because the restaurant has become part of who they are. Status isn't about more discounts — it's about recognition, exclusivity, and access that money can't buy. A "Regular" tag means nothing. Being personally invited to the new menu tasting before it opens to the public — that means everything. This is the layer that creates the customers who bring their friends, post about the restaurant unprompted, and never churn.
Structure — what status feels like
Reserved table during peak hours without booking.
Name on the "regulars board" (physical or digital).
First access to new menu items before public launch.
Invited to private events, tastings, chef's table nights.
Personal WhatsApp from the manager/owner on birthday.
Cost to restaurant: near zero. Value to customer: infinite.

The hook model — applied to your platform

1. Trigger
External: QR code at table, WhatsApp "you're 1 away" message, Instagram story mention by friend.
Internal (built over time): "I feel like visiting Smoor today" — the brand becomes the trigger itself.
2. Action
Visit, scan QR, complete activity. The action must be simpler than the reward feels. Friction kills this step — one tap to check in, no login, no form.
3. Variable reward
Sometimes it's a milestone completion. Sometimes it's a surprise bonus on an unexpected visit. Sometimes it's a personal message. The variability keeps anticipation alive.
4. Investment
The customer builds progress they don't want to abandon. Their journey history, their unlocked loops, their status tier. The more they invest, the more costly it is to leave. This is the moat.

Reward logic by customer level — not all customers are equal

Level 0
First-time visitor (never been before)
They have zero emotional investment. They don't know if they'll return. The loyalty system must do two things: surprise them with something they didn't expect, and make the next visit feel inevitable — like they'd be leaving something on the table if they didn't come back.
What to give
Instant welcome reward on scan: "Welcome! Your first visit already counts — you're 1/5 toward a free [item]."
Low-cost, high-perceived-value item suggestion: free drink upgrade, complimentary dessert sample.
Never ask them to "complete your profile" on first visit. Just give them the win.
Level 1
Early returner (2–4 visits)
They've come back once or twice but the habit isn't formed yet. They're in the "testing" phase — deciding if this place is worth returning to. The loyalty system must increase visit frequency here. Goal gradient is most powerful at this stage — show them they're close.
What to give + when
Keep the milestone loop front and center — progress bar is the hero element.
Introduce one engagement activity: "Leave a Google review and unlock a free coffee today."
At 60% completion: WhatsApp trigger fires ("2 more visits and your free [item] unlocks").
Don't offer too many activities yet — one clear path is better than five.
Level 2
Habitual visitor (5–15 visits, completed first loop)
The habit is forming. They've completed at least one loop. They're starting to feel like regulars. This is where you must evolve the experience — if the program feels the same as when they started, engagement decays. New journey must unlock with fanfare.
What to give + how to escalate
Celebrate loop completion: full-screen moment, animated unlock, "You've been coming here for 2 months — you're officially a regular."
Unlock engagement activities: social sharing, referral, event participation.
Introduce category-specific Loop 2 (brunch visits, cocktail orders, etc.)
Add variable element: occasional surprise "mystery reward" to maintain dopamine loop.
Level 3
Loyal regular (15+ visits, multiple loops)
These are your most valuable customers — they drive 65–80% of restaurant revenue despite being a minority of visitors. The risk here is program fatigue — if there's nothing new to earn, they disengage. Rewards at this level must shift from transactional to experiential and status-based.
What to give — shift from discounts to access
Stop giving discounts. They don't need them — they already come here.
Give access: early menu previews, private events, chef interaction.
Give recognition: "You've been with us for 6 months" milestone, personal manager message.
Give exclusivity: a reward tier only they can see. Make it feel earned and rare.
Trigger referral activity strongly here — loyal regulars have the most credible word-of-mouth.
Level 4
Lapsed customer (no visit in 30+ days)
They didn't leave because they hate you — they just forgot, or life got in the way. Research shows 60% of lapsed customers will return with the right trigger at the right time. The cost of winning them back is a fraction of acquiring a new customer. Loss aversion works in your favor here — remind them what they'd be missing.
Win-back sequence
Day 30: "We haven't seen you in a while — your [X] journey is still waiting for you."
Day 45: "Your progress is still saved — come back this week and pick up where you left off."
Day 60: Final offer — a genuine "comeback reward." A specific, time-limited, high-value item.
If no response after 60 days: archive. Don't keep messaging — it becomes spam.

What to reward — and what not to

Reward this
Behaviors that directly benefit the restaurant
Every reward you give should either drive a visit, increase spend, or amplify marketing. If a behavior does none of those things, the reward is charity — not strategy.
High-value behaviors to reward
Visit frequency — core of every loyalty program. Reward the repeat.
Off-peak visits — double reward on Mon/Tue/Wed. Smooths revenue curve.
Google/Zomato review — direct SEO and discovery value. High reward justified.
Instagram story with location tag — free marketing to their followers.
Friend referral who actually visits — highest-value acquisition you can get.
Birthday visit — creates a ritual and brings a group.
Event participation — drives covers on otherwise slow event nights.
Higher spend visit — "upgrade your order to unlock a bonus reward."
Never reward this
Behaviors that cost you without compounding value
These seem generous but train customers to behave in ways that hurt your business — or reward behaviors they'd do anyway, wasting your reward budget.
Behaviors to avoid rewarding
Signing up alone (no visit) — rewards people for doing nothing. Inflate database with ghosts.
Negative reviews — obvious but make sure your system can't be gamed this way.
Delivery orders (if you want dine-in) — unless your goal IS delivery. Reward the channel you want.
Just following on Instagram — no visit intent, no behavior change.
Complaints/feedback forms alone — gameable and breeds complaint behavior.
Sharing another restaurant's post — always check activity rules for gaming potential.
Reward type ladder — what to give at each stage
Match reward type to customer maturity
The type of reward matters as much as the trigger. Early customers need tangible value. Mature customers need status and experience. Getting this wrong is the most common loyalty mistake — giving discounts to regulars who'd visit anyway.
The reward type ladder
Visits 1–5 (trust building): Free item on the menu. Tangible, immediate, clear value. Perceived value high, actual cost low (₹80–₹150).

Visits 6–15 (habit phase): % discounts, free upgrades, combo deals. Encourage higher spend alongside the reward.

Visits 15–30 (regular phase): Exclusive early access. First to know about new menu items. Skip-the-queue on busy nights. Priority reservation.

Visits 30+ (ambassador phase): Experiences — private tastings, chef's table, name on the wall, special anniversary recognition. Never discount these customers — they'll pay full price anyway.

Social activities (any phase): Match reward to the marketing value. Google review (massive SEO value) = free drink. Reel with 1000+ views = exclusive invite. Scale reward to ROI.
Cost vs perceived value — the math
Rewards that feel big but cost little
The best rewards are those where the gap between actual cost and perceived value is widest. A 50% discount feels enormous but costs you margin on every item. A "reserved table at the best seat in the house" costs you nothing but feels extremely special.
High perceived value / low actual cost rewards
Chef's table experience (10-person monthly) → costs 1 evening, creates 10 ambassador stories.
Birthday message from the chef/owner personally → costs 2 minutes, creates lifelong loyalty.
"First look" at the new menu before launch → costs nothing, makes them feel VIP.
Early reservation access before public booking → costs nothing operationally.
Complimentary amuse-bouche on arrival (if you're premium) → ₹50 cost, feels like ₹500 gesture.
Name on the "regulars board" → ₹0 cost, identity-level attachment.

When to reward — timing is everything

Immediately
Rewards that must fire within seconds
Any delay between behavior and reward weakens the psychological connection. Skinner's research showed that immediate reinforcement creates the strongest habit loops. If a customer checks in and gets a WhatsApp message 3 days later, it's meaningless. If they get it while still at the restaurant, it's magic.
Must be instant (within 60 seconds)
First scan / sign-up → welcome message + progress shown.
Milestone completion (5th visit) → reward unlocks with notification while still in restaurant.
Activity submission (review/story) → "Received! Your reward is being processed" confirmation.
Referral friend visits → referrer notified same day.
Birthday date → message fires at 9am on their birthday, not a week later.
Anticipatory
Messages sent before the reward moment to build anticipation
Dopamine spikes on anticipation, not just receipt. Sending a message before a reward unlocks creates a "looking forward to it" emotion that makes the actual visit more enjoyable. This is why Swiggy shows your delivery driver on a map — the anticipation is part of the experience.
Anticipatory trigger windows
At 60% loop progress: "You're getting close — 2 more [coffees/visits/cocktails] to go."
3 days before birthday: "Your birthday's coming up — we have something special for you."
Week before a recurring event: "Our Sunday jazz brunch is back — your loyalty discount is waiting."
When a new journey unlocks: "A new challenge just opened for you — explore it."
Urgency-based
Time-limited rewards that activate loss aversion
"Your reward is available" gets ignored. "Your reward expires in 72 hours" gets action. Loss aversion means the fear of losing something is twice as motivating as the excitement of gaining the same thing. Every reward should have an expiry — not punishingly short (3 days is too aggressive for monthly diners), but real enough to feel urgent.
Optimal expiry windows by restaurant type
Coffee shop (daily habit): 7–14 days. Customers come frequently anyway.
Casual dining (weekly habit): 21–30 days. Enough time to plan a visit.
Fine dining/resto-bar (monthly habit): 45–60 days. Respect that they may only visit monthly.
One-time event rewards (e.g. "bring a friend this weekend"): 5–7 days. Short urgency is fine for specific campaigns.

Always: send an expiry reminder 5 days before. "Your [reward] expires on [date]."
Never send at these moments
Timing mistakes that kill trust
Timing to avoid
During meal service hours (1–3pm, 8–10pm) — nobody checks loyalty messages when eating.
Monday morning — lowest dining intent day of the week.
Multiple messages in one day — more than 2 WhatsApp messages in a day = muted or blocked.
Generic campaigns on festival days everyone is messaging on (Diwali, New Year) — gets lost in noise.

Best send windows (India specific):
Weekday campaigns: Tuesday–Thursday, 11am or 6pm.
Weekend nudges: Friday 4pm ("plans for tonight?").
Birthday messages: 8–9am on the day itself.

The 8 things that kill loyalty programs — avoid all of them

Killer 1
Rewards that feel too far away
If the first reward requires 10+ visits, casual diners quit after 3. Research shows the reward must feel achievable within the customer's natural visit cycle. For a restaurant visited monthly, a 10-visit milestone is a 10-month journey — that's not loyalty, that's a test of patience.
Rule
First reward must be achievable within the customer's natural visit frequency × 2. Monthly diners: first reward in 3 visits. Weekly cafe visitors: first reward in 5 visits. Daily coffee: first reward in 7 days.
Killer 2
Only rewarding with discounts
Discount-only programs train customers to expect deals and feel cheated when they pay full price. They attract deal-hunters — the least loyal customer segment — and devalue your brand. If every visit ends in a discount, your "normal price" starts to feel like a ripoff.
Rule
Maximum 30% of your rewards should be discounts. The rest: free items, access, experiences, recognition. Discounts are acceptable in early loops and win-back campaigns only.
Killer 3
Showing an empty state after sign-up
The first screen after joining the loyalty program must never be blank or say "0 points." This is a morale killer. Research on endowed progress is definitive: showing a 0% progress bar is worse than not showing progress at all. The customer immediately feels they've achieved nothing.
Rule
First scan = Visit 1 already counted. Progress bar shows 1/5 (or whatever the loop is). The program started the moment they walked in. Make this visually celebrated — not neutral.
Killer 4
Too many activities shown at once
The paradox of choice: when customers see 8 activities they can complete, they feel overwhelmed and do nothing. This is well-documented in behavioral economics (Iyengar & Lepper's jam study). Three options creates urgency and focus. Eight creates paralysis.
Rule
Maximum 3 active activities visible to a customer at any time. Rotate them as they complete or expire. Give each one a "deadline" to create urgency. "Share a story this week to unlock your reward" beats "share a story anytime."
Killer 5
No feedback between action and reward
Customer posts a story, submits it, sees... nothing. No confirmation, no timeline, no next step. They assume it didn't work, give up, and never do it again. Silence after an action is the fastest way to kill engagement.
Rule
Every submission must get an immediate "we got it" response. Even if verification takes 24 hours: "Your story has been submitted — your reward will unlock within 2 hours once verified." Progress state keeps them engaged even when nothing has happened yet.
Killer 6
Program goes stale after first few months
47% of loyalty members engage less after 3 months because the program never changes. If the same 5 activities and same milestone loop are available 6 months later, the program signals "we stopped caring." Regulars need novelty to stay engaged.
Rule
Rotate at least one activity or campaign per month. Seasonal events (Monsoon special, Diwali offer) keep it feeling fresh. Surprise "double reward weekend" once a quarter. New journey unlocking for long-term members. The program must feel alive.
Killer 7
Complicated redemption at the counter
The research is brutal here: if redemption takes more than 15 seconds at the counter, staff start avoiding it. Customer shows their phone, staff look confused, manager gets called, queue builds. Program dies from embarrassment within weeks. This has killed more loyalty programs than any digital issue.
Rule
Staff-side UI: one screen, one tap. Counter staff need to see the coupon code, hit confirm, done. Train staff for 10 minutes, not 2 hours. The redemption flow must be designed for a busy Friday night, not a calm Tuesday afternoon.
Killer 8
Rewarding what customers would do anyway
Giving a reward for a behavior the customer already does is wasteful — it trains them to expect payment for something that was free. If a customer already posts on Instagram regularly, giving them a reward for it doesn't change their behavior. Your reward budget should only target behavior change.
Rule
Before adding any reward, ask: "Would this customer do this even without the reward?" If yes — either remove it, make the reward much smaller, or redesign it to target a higher-effort version of the same behavior (e.g. not "post a photo" but "post a reel with our location tag and at least 10 story views").

Master blueprint — works for most Indian restaurants

Step 1 — Define your restaurant type first
High-frequency
Coffee, QSR, fast casual. Visit weekly or daily. Goal: increase frequency + spend.
Mid-frequency
Casual dining, cafe, brunch spots. Visit 2–4× month. Goal: occasion ownership.
Low-frequency
Fine dining, resto-bar. Visit monthly or less. Goal: maximize each visit value + social amplification.
Step 2 — Set your loop structure by type
High-frequency (cafe, coffee, QSR):
Loop 1: Every 7 visits → free item of choice. Start at 1/7 on first scan.
Loop 2 (unlocks after L1): 3 weekend visits in a row → free size upgrade every Sunday for 1 month.
Activity: Leave a review = free pastry today. Refer a friend = free coffee for both.
Expiry: 14 days on all rewards.

Mid-frequency (casual dining, brunch, bar):
Loop 1: Every 5 visits → free starter or dessert. Start at 1/5.
Loop 2: 3 Saturday brunches → complimentary brunch for 2 (once).
Loop 3: Spend ₹2000+ on 3 visits → sommelier/cocktail pairing experience.
Activity: Instagram story = 20% off next visit. Refer group of 4+ = free round of drinks.
Expiry: 30 days on all rewards.

Low-frequency (fine dining, premium resto-bar):
Loop 1: Every 3 visits → complimentary amuse-bouche course on next visit.
Loop 2: 5 visits within 6 months → Chef's table invitation (exclusive).
Activity: Write a detailed review (100+ words) = exclusive early access to seasonal menu.
Status: After 10 visits → reserved regular status, personal server assigned, name known.
Expiry: 60 days. No urgency spam — this audience hates it.
Note: Never offer discounts at this level. Access and recognition only.
Step 3 — The 5 mandatory WhatsApp triggers (every restaurant)
T1 — Welcome Fires on first scan. "Welcome to [Restaurant]! Your first visit is already counted — 4 more and your [reward] is yours."

T2 — 60% nudge At 60% loop completion. "You're getting close! [X] more [visits/orders] and [reward] unlocks."

T3 — Reward unlocked Fires the moment milestone completes. "[Customer name], your [reward] is ready! Show this message at the counter anytime in the next [X] days."

T4 — Expiry warning 5 days before reward expires. "Your [reward] expires [date]. Come in before then so you don't miss it."

T5 — Win-back (day 30) "Haven't seen you in a while! Your [journey] is still waiting — come back this week and pick up where you left off."
Step 4 — The activity set (choose 3 to start with)
Tier A (highest ROI — always include):
Google review — reward: free item worth ₹150–250. ROI: massive SEO impact, review lasts forever.
Friend referral (friend must actually visit) — reward: free item for both. ROI: lowest-cost acquisition channel.

Tier B (high marketing value):
Instagram story with location tag — reward: 20% off same/next visit. ROI: immediate social reach.
Birthday visit (advance notice) — reward: free dessert + personal message. ROI: group visit (4+ covers typical).

Tier C (engagement + frequency):
Off-peak visit (weekday lunch/dinner) — double progress on milestone. ROI: smooths revenue curve.
Event participation — reward: exclusive invite to next event. ROI: fills event nights, creates regulars.
Step 5 — The 3 numbers every restaurant must track
Loop completion %
What % of customers complete their first milestone loop. Target: >40%. Below 25% = reward too far or no reminders.
Return rate
Of customers who scan, what % return within 30 days. Target: >50%. Below 30% = welcome experience needs fixing.
Redemption rate
Of rewards generated, what % get redeemed. Target: >60%. Below 40% = rewards aren't compelling or expiry is too long.
The universal rule that overrides everything
A loyalty program is not a discount system. It is a behavior change system. Every element — every reward, every trigger, every loop — must be designed to change one specific behavior. More visits. Higher spend. Social sharing. Friend referral. If you can't name the behavior a reward is changing, remove the reward. The best programs feel generous to customers and profitable to restaurants — because they only give rewards that generate more than they cost.
